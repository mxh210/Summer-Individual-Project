# Reproduction Comparisons
## Table 1 - Missing values
Table 1 was reproduced by calculating missing-value percentages from the raw UCI diabetic readmission dataset after replacing `"?"` values with missing values. The overall missing-data pattern was consistent with the core paper: `weight`, `payer_code`, and `medical_specialty` were the only features with substantial missingness. `weight` was 96.86% missing in the reproduction, closely matching the 97% reported in the paper. `payer_code` was 39.56% missing, which differs from the 52% shown in Table 1 but closely matches the 40% stated later in the paper’s preprocessing section. `medical_specialty` was 49.08% missing, compared with 53% in Table 1 and 47% in the preprocessing text. These differences are likely due to differences between the public UCI dataset and the dataset version used by the authors, as well as minor inconsistencies within the paper itself. The discrepancy is unlikely to materially affect the reproduction because `weight` and `payer_code` are removed from further analysis, while `medical_specialty` is retained using a missing-value category, following the original study.

## Table 3 - Distribution of variable values and readmissions
Table 3 was largely reproduced successfully. The distribution of patients across HbA1c status, gender, discharge disposition, admission source, medical specialty, primary diagnosis, race, and age closely matched the core paper, with most percentages matching exactly or differing by only 0.1 percentage points. This suggests that the preprocessing and categorical grouping steps were implemented correctly. However, the reproduced 30-day readmission rates were consistently slightly lower than those reported in the paper. The reproduced final dataset contained 69,987 encounters compared with 69,984 in the paper, but the number of readmitted cases was 6,285 compared with 6,459 in the paper. This gives an overall readmission rate of 8.98%, compared with 9.23% in the original study. The difference is small in absolute terms, around 0.25 percentage points overall, but it appears systematically across most groups. Since the population distributions match closely, the discrepancy is unlikely to be caused by incorrect category grouping. A more likely explanation is that the public UCI dataset’s `readmitted` labels differ slightly from the readmission outcome calculated by the original authors from the full Health Facts database. This should be noted as a minor limitation of the reproduction.

## Table 4 - Coefficients of noninteraction terms estimated from the final logistic regression model
Table 4 was reproduced closely. The estimated coefficients were generally very similar to the original paper, with the same direction of association across the main variables. Most significance conclusions were also consistent, including race `Other`, admission `Other`, missing medical specialty, time in hospital, musculoskeletal diagnosis, and HbA1c `high, changed`. Two rows differed in whether they crossed the paper’s p < 0.01 significance threshold: respiratory diagnosis was not significant in the original paper but was significant in the reproduction, while HbA1c `high, not changed` was significant in the original paper but not significant in the reproduction. However, both rows had p-values close to 0.01 in both analyses, and the coefficient estimates remained similar. These differences are therefore likely due to small differences in the public UCI dataset, the slightly lower number of readmitted cases observed in the reproduction, and minor implementation differences in model fitting rather than a substantive failure to reproduce the model.
